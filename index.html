What you are looking at

An ecosystem, not a rule

Most cellular automata apply one fixed rule everywhere. This one doesn't. Each coloured cell is an organism with its own small genome that decides how it moves, eats and breeds. When it breeds, the child gets a slightly mutated copy. Creatures that make bad choices starve or get eaten; those that make good ones fill the grid with their descendants. That is natural selection, running live.

Green-to-violet cells are grazers. Their hue is a gene that does nothing, so it works as a family name: watch patches of one colour spread as a lineage takes over, then get replaced. Deep red cells are hunters, brightening toward orange when well fed. The glow is trail: a chemical grazers leave where they have eaten, which spreads and fades exactly like the trail in slime-mould models. It takes the colour of the lineage that laid it, so a network glowing in one hue is one family's territory. The dim background is food, regrowing over a fixed landscape of fertile valleys and barren ridges.

Conway's Game of Life, and its cousins. Every cell looks at its eight neighbours and, by one rule applied everywhere at once, decides whether it lives or dies. Newborn cells flash warm, long-lived cells cool towards blue. Try the rule buttons: HighLife has a replicator, Day & Night is symmetric between life and death, Seeds explodes.

Wolfram's elementary automata. A single row of cells, each looking only at itself and its two neighbours, evolves downward through time. There are exactly 256 such rules. Rule 30 is chaotic and was once used as a random number generator; rule 90 draws the Sierpinski triangle; rule 110 can compute anything a computer can.

The rigorous version
The ecology as an asynchronous cellular automaton

A toroidal lattice with three layers: a food scalar with logistic regrowth toward a static fertility map; a trail scalar obeying discrete diffusion (five-point Laplacian, coefficient 0.18) with geometric evaporation (3% per step); and an occupancy layer with at most one organism per cell. Each step, every organism acts once in a freshly shuffled order (as in Dewdney's Wa-Tor, 1984): pay a metabolic cost, eat, deposit, move to the best-scoring free neighbour, split if energy exceeds a genetically set threshold, die at zero energy.

Genomes are five floats in [0,1] with Gaussian mutation (σ = 0.06) reflected at the boundaries. Gene 4 is behaviourally inert by construction and serves as both the colour and the drift control in the experiments below. Full definitions are in ecology.js and the methods note in docs/methods.md.

What is tested, and against what

Determinism (same seed, same fingerprint after hundreds of steps). Invariants every step: one organism per cell, positive energy, genes in range. Diffusion conserves mass with evaporation off and is symmetric. Evaporation is geometric at the configured rate. Grazers starve without food; hunters starve without grazers; grazers fill the land without hunters. For the classical worlds: gliders translate (1,1) every 4 generations across the torus edge, the R-pentomino stabilises at generation 1103 with population 116 as LifeWiki records, rule 30's first rows match Wolfram's table, rule 90 is XOR of its neighbours. The test directory is the ground truth.

The loop

How this project builds itself

Every change here went through the same cycle: an issue is chosen, a branch is cut, code and tests are written, a pull request is opened, continuous integration runs the tests and a quick pass of every experiment, the change is merged, the site redeploys, and a retrospective is written. Then the retrospective seeds the next issues. An AI agent does all of it. A human started it and can stop it. There have been … cycles so far.

Why do it this way? Because it is the ordinary discipline of software engineering, made visible. Nothing in the loop is exotic. The point is that it is followed, every time, by a machine, and that you can audit every step: click any issue or pull request below. The protocol is written down in LOOP.md.
